Six defects, zero prints: software-defined manufacturing vs. an AI CAD toolkit

2026-09-25 · Dan Newcome · full technical write-up on dnuke.com

An assembly defined in code can be checked in code, before a single tool touches material. That's the bet behind PUNKFAB. Every defect caught in software is a print, a setup or a tool you never burn. To see what that's worth, we took one real mechanism and ran it through two pipelines. The first was text-to-cad, the most popular way to have an AI agent do CAD right now: about 16k stars in five months, MIT licensed, thirteen agent skills. The second was our own define → simulate → verify loop. Between them they found six defects, and nothing got printed.

The test piece

A two-cell variable chain motor, after Tada et al., IROS 2025 (doi:10.1109/IROS60139.2025.11246199). Two ordinary drone motors get spur gears on both rotor and stator, mesh, and run as one motor from one driver and one encoder. The second motor rides a link that swings around the first, so the motor can wrap an elbow instead of sitting beside it. It's six printed parts with real kinematics, a physics claim worth proving, and small features that can go wrong: a fair test of any pipeline.

Both pipelines sit on the same CAD kernel (build123d on OpenCASCADE), and the text-to-cad build reused our geometry unchanged. So this compares process, not modeling skill.

01 / Define: the constraint comes first

In our loop a mechanism starts as parameters and a model of what it has to do, before there's any geometry. For the chain motor, that model reproduces the paper's published torque and speed table to within 0.2%. And “define” means the whole assembly, not a part. The six parts, how they mate, and the gear couplings the simulator runs all come from the same constants, so when the model changes, the assembly changes with it. That's test-driven fabrication: write the constraint, then converge until the build goes green.

02 / Simulate: three defects no CAD tool can see

Half of what matters about this motor isn't in its geometry. Our MuJoCo model loads the exported parts on a real joint tree, with the gear meshes as constraints, and put three questions to it:

  • The wiring has to be reversed. The gear mesh spins the second motor backwards, so two of its phase leads must be swapped. Wired straight, the two motors cancel to zero output torque: a motor that draws current and doesn't move. The paper doesn't mention it.
  • The bend has to be torque-neutral, and it is, but only if the gear ratios match. At stall the hinge feels 0.000 mN·m. Get one stator gear a single tooth wrong and it feels 0.401 mN·m, exactly what virtual work predicts. Leave the stator gears off and a 155° bend scrambles commutation completely.
  • Bending fast loads the controller. The second rotor turns twice the hinge angle, so the output servo must supply 2·I·φ̈ while the elbow accelerates. The sim matches the formula exactly, and now the controller spec includes it.

text-to-cad has no simulate stage. Its kinematics drive a viewer's pose sliders and never move geometry for checking, and nothing in it runs dynamics. None of these three would have surfaced before a real motor sat on the bench and didn't turn.

06 / Verify: three defects we'd have printed

Here the AI CAD toolkit earned its reputation. Its clearance query and printability tool found three manufacturing defects in parts that had passed all our geometry checks:

  • Zero gear backlash. Our overlap check counts touching as passing. text-to-cad's clearance query measured 0.000 mm between the teeth, and printed gears like that bind. The fix is now a parameter: the centre distance opens for 0.1 mm of backlash per mesh.
  • 0.11 mm walls. Screw holes broke into a bore in the hub and the base. Both parts were perfectly valid solids, just not printable. The hub now keys into the base with a hex instead.
  • A cap that needs support in every orientation. Turning the fused dowel into a press-fit steel pin took it from 13–22% support to 0%.

Its "look at a render after every change" rule caught one more thing, in its own kinematics. Our first mate declaration left half the motor behind at a 90° bend, with no error. Anything that can be wrong silently has to be checked, not trusted.

What this says about the method

A CAD tool answers "can this be made". A manufacturing loop also has to answer "does it work". text-to-cad is a strong define stage: a fast, cached build system, good errors, and excellent visual review. But a green render isn't a working motor. Of the six defects, three were physics and needed a simulator. The other three were geometry that our checks hadn't yet been told to look for.

The second half is the real selling point: checks compound. Every defect found once becomes a permanent constraint, so the three text-to-cad found are becoming standing rules. Gear-mesh clearance already runs on every build, and minimum-wall rules guard the features that failed. Sampled wall thickness and support-by-orientation from its MIT-licensed printability tool are next. Borrowing is what open tooling is for. A traditional shop learns these lessons by scrapping parts, one job at a time. A software-defined shop learns each one once.

That's what makes sweeping affordable. Backlash, hub geometry and pin fit are parameters now, not fixes. The next step is to sweep them across a family of motors and let the constraints pick the survivors, rather than guess one design and hope.

The simulation is a cache of reality. The kinematic and torque results are measured against the published paper. The efficiency curves still use placeholder loss values, and they stay flagged as such until the bench measures them.
